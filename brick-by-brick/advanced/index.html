How this works. Both paths are handed the same money every month — your rent plus whatever you can put aside. The renter pays rent and invests the rest. The builder pays that same rent until they move in, buys the plot as soon as they can afford it, and puts everything left into the house; once they move in the rent stops, the running costs of owning start, and what is freed up finishes the house and then goes into the same investment. Progress is tracked as a share of the house rather than a running total of shillings, so a build cost rising faster than your budget shows up as a house that never gets finished — not as a finish date quietly extrapolated past the horizon. Net worth at year 25 is the plot and the house at what they would fetch, less selling costs and any tax, plus whatever is in the pot.

What the money doesn't capture. A landlord can raise your rent or ask you to leave; a finished house cannot do either. For most families this is also the only asset they will ever hold that a bank will lend against, and the discipline of building is savings that cannot be spent on something else. If the two lines come out close, those are the things that decide it.

Not modelled: a construction loan or mortgage part-way through, land disputes and title problems, materials bought in bulk ahead of a price rise, family labour, or renting out part of the plot while you build. Growth rates here are steady averages; real ones arrive in lumps, and so does the money to build with.

This is a model, not advice. It does what you tell it to. The numbers you put in matter far more than the arithmetic it does with them.
