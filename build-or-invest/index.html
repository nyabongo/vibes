How this works. Both paths start with the same lump sum. The build path pays for the land on day one and draws the rest of the project cost evenly over the construction months; whatever hasn't been spent yet keeps earning the investment return, and so does every shilling of rent the finished building throws off. While it's under construction the building is worth what you've sunk into it — no more. Once complete it's worth a year's stabilised net income divided by your exit yield, which is where a development actually makes or loses its money. Net worth at year 10 is that value less selling costs and capital gains tax, plus the cash pot.

Why the lump sum doesn't move the verdict. It enters both paths identically, so it cancels out of the gap between them — change it and the headline number stays put. What it decides is whether you can fund the thing at all, which is what the warning above is for. To change the answer, move the rent, the exit yield, the build cost or the market return.

Not modelled: construction finance or interest during the build, phased or pre-sold units, selling units off individually, VAT and capital allowances, or the cost of a project that stalls. Drawdown is even across the build months, and a finished building is priced on stabilised income from its first month even while it's still filling. Land rates are charged on site value only. Rent growth and investment returns are steady averages here; real ones arrive in lumps, and a half-built block earns nothing while you argue with a contractor. If the project costs more than you have, the gap is charged at the same rate your investments earn — a stand-in for finance you'd actually have to go and raise.

This is a model, not advice. It does what you tell it to. The numbers you put in matter far more than the arithmetic it does with them.
